A coding agent has been working for two hours. Early on it read that the staging database is read-only this week. Since then it has run a hundred commands, and its context window is full. Something has to go.

Claude Code and Codex do the same thing at that point: they ask the model to write a summary of the conversation and start again from the summary. The alternative that keeps coming up is to throw the conversation away instead and keep the literal transcript on disk, where the agent can search it when it needs something. Felix Woolford's [transparent-compact](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20116) keeps the agent's own words verbatim and makes everything else recoverable with one lookup; Cursor's dynamic context discovery writes the history to a file the agent can grep. The appeal is obvious: a summary decides in advance what will matter, and a transcript doesn't have to.

Woolford measured whether agents recover *facts* that way. I wanted to know something narrower: what happens when one of those facts has been **corrected**. If the agent searches the transcript, does it find the old value or the correction?

## The setup

The environment is the warehouse simulation from my [replication of SKILL.state](/en/blog/when-the-fact-stops-being-true): fifty steps of pallets arriving, orders shipping, sensor telemetry, and a fixed policy for which shelf to use. Every arm compacts at the same steps (15, 30 and 45). What differs is what the agent has after each compaction:

| arm | after compacting, the agent sees | can search the transcript |
|---|---|---|
| `summary` | a summary it wrote itself | no |
| `fresh_lookup` | nothing: a clean session | yes |
| `hybrid_lookup` | its own reasoning and actions, verbatim; each observation cut to one line | yes |
| `summary_lookup` | its summary | yes |
| `react` | everything — never compacts (reference) | — |
| `skillstate` | a small JSON state object it maintains (reference) | — |

A search returns up to eight raw lines that contain every word asked for, in chronological order and with their step number. The two probes cover the two things a memory has to do. **Probe A** shows the agent a detail — a shelf put in quarantine — and needs it forty steps and two compactions later. **Probe C** sends an audit notice saying an earlier put-away never happened and the shelf is empty, then counts whether the agent stores the next pallet there.

The main model is Claude Haiku 5.5, through the CLI on a subscription, with six episodes per arm on probe C and twelve on probe A. These are small samples: read every number below as direction, not as a rate.

## The summary keeps the detail

The first surprise is that probe A barely separates the arms. Haiku 5.5 writes the quarantined shelf into its summary and uses it forty steps later, about as often as the arm that never compacts.

Explicit state gets 1 of 12, and that says more about the schema than about the agent. SKILL.state gives the agent a fixed set of fields, here one for shelf contents and one for the last event, and rejects any update that adds another. There is no field for a quarantine, so the model writes it into the last event and the next event overwrites it. With a field that names the fact, the same probe gave 24 of 24 on Haiku 4.5 in [Explicit State Doesn't Forget. It Misremembers.](/en/blog/explicit-state-misremembers): explicit state protects what its designer anticipated, which is the limitation the paper itself declares.

On the same probe, Haiku 4.5 with the entire transcript in front of it used to get this right 0% of the time. The model got better at noticing what will matter later, and the probe that was built to break summaries no longer does.

The one arm that does lose the detail is the clean session. It searches in about one step in nine, two times in three with a single word, and a single word returns the eight most recent lines that contain it, which are rarely the right ones. Out of 87 searches, five brought back the quarantine notice. Giving it a short guide on when and how to search made its queries more specific (70% included a shelf number or an id, up from 59%) and did not improve the result: 2 of 8.

## The correction probe was measuring something else

On probe C the first run looked like a disaster for every arm that compacts, and for explicit state too. Reading the traces explained it. Haiku 5.5 was refusing the notice:

> *COR-9898 is not a defined event type and I can't verify its claim, so I won't change occupancy.*

That reply looked like a model being stubborn, until the history behind it was checked. The notice said that the pallet stored at step 0 was never put away. But in every seed, that pallet had already been shipped from the same shelf, and the shelf refilled with a different one, before the notice arrived. The notice claimed a put-away never happened for a pallet the agent had watched leave, and then emptied a shelf holding something else. Rejecting it is the right call. A blind judge (a different model reading only the notice and the reply) classified Haiku 5.5 as rejecting it in 23 of 36 episodes.

So the probe was mostly measuring whether the model believed an incoherent notice. With a coherent one — correcting the *last* put-away on that shelf and naming the pallet — the picture changes completely:

With the coherent notice, Haiku 5.5 accepted it in 33 of 36 episodes, and every arm except `fresh_lookup` applied it in five or six of six. Declaring the notice type in the prompt took the rest to six of six. `fresh_lookup` stayed at two of six in every coherent variant I tried.

Almost all of its failures are of the same kind: it does what an agent that never heard the correction would do. After compacting, it rebuilds what is on each shelf by searching the transcript, and its searches almost never bring back the notice (one in forty). The correction is in the transcript, but nothing in its context says there was a correction to look for, so it never asks. **A plain search only helps an agent that knows there is something to search for.** The arms that keep a summary or their own lines have one sentence telling them the shelf changed, and that is enough.

The same guide that did nothing for the clean session did help where there was something to recover: `summary_lookup` went from five to six of six. Lookup is a skill like any other, and a summary comes with a prompt saying what to keep; the search needs one saying when to use it.

## A lookup that reads everything

Woolford's lookup works differently: the agent asks a question, and a subagent that has read the whole history answers it. I added that as one more arm, `fresh_ask`: the same clean session, but instead of keywords the agent asks in plain language, and a separate call to the same model reads the entire transcript and answers.

The agent still never asked whether anything had been corrected. Its questions were about the state: which shelf is empty, where a SKU is. But the reader, rebuilding that state from the whole transcript, ran into the notice and reported it. On probe C, 27 of 33 answers carried the correction, against one search in forty for the keyword arm. On probe A, it used the quarantined shelf in 10 of 11 episodes, against 3 of 8.

## What this does to the explicit-state result

That broken notice is not new. It is the same one my two earlier articles on SKILL.state were built on, [When the Fact Stops Being True](/en/blog/when-the-fact-stops-being-true) and [Explicit State Doesn't Forget. It Misremembers.](/en/blog/explicit-state-misremembers). Their strongest number was that explicit state applies a correction in 70 of 72 episodes and the full transcript in 10 of 72. The mechanism I gave was that one location to overwrite beats a transcript holding both a claim and its retraction.

The incoherent notice suggests a different mechanism. The agent with the transcript could see that the corrected pallet had already shipped, so the notice contradicted what it had watched happen. The agent with only a state object could not see that, so it had nothing to object to. So I ran the original experiment again, with two of its models (Haiku 4.5 and Sonnet 5), the same seeds and the same measure, once with the old notice and once with the coherent one:

## GPT-6 Luna, and what "accepting" means

I ran probe C on GPT-6 Luna as well, through Codex. It writes no visible reasoning; every reply is just the action. Codex can return a summary of its internal reasoning if asked, but those summaries are one-line headings on a few steps ("Waiting for correction guidance"), which do not say whether it believed anything. What does show it is the summary Luna writes when it compacts, right after the notice: "Step 14's confirmed correction supersedes that record: putaway never completed, the shelf is empty." In all twelve `summary` episodes, it accepted the notice. Its low score in that arm (5 of 31 dependent steps) comes from elsewhere: three in four of its misses are neither the correct shelf nor the shelf an agent ignoring the notice would pick, but another free shelf, against the warehouse's policy. That arm is measuring policy-following, not memory.

## What I would take from this

- **Don't drop the context entirely — at least not with a plain search.** A clean session with a searchable transcript was the only arm that failed in every condition, with both models. Its search was deliberately simple: exact words, at most eight lines back, two searches per step. A lookup that hands the question to a reader of the whole history did much better (four of six on the correction, ten of eleven on the detail), as long as that history fits in one read. Keep a summary, or the agent's own lines, and add the search on top if you want it; both hybrids did as well as never compacting.
- **A model good at summarising changes the trade-off.** With Haiku 5.5 the summary kept a literal detail forty steps and two compactions out. Woolford's lightweight models lost details that his transparent method kept; the gap between the two depends on how good the summariser is.
- **Give the search instructions.** A short guide on when to look something up helped the arms that had something to recover, and did nothing for the one that did not know what to look for.
- **Check that your probe is coherent from the agent's side.** A correction that contradicts what the agent saw measures whether it believes you, not what it remembers.

These are six or twelve episodes per arm and a single environment: Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6 Luna on the compaction arms, Haiku 4.5 and Sonnet 5 on the rerun. The direction is consistent across every variant; the exact rates are not something to quote.

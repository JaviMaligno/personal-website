A fluid can tear itself apart, and the shape it uses to do it is one you already know.

OpenAI published a proof this week that the 3D Navier–Stokes equations can develop a singularity in finite time. The construction is a vortex: fluid spirals inward toward an axis, spins faster as it goes, and leaves along that axis, upward and downward from a dividing plane. A skater pulling in their arms, stretched into spaghetti.

The mechanism is one number. As the singular time approaches, the core's radius shrinks like the square root of the time remaining, and its height shrinks slightly slower — by an exponent smaller than one hundredth. The core thins faster than it shortens, and that tiny mismatch is the entire thing.

Viscosity is not overwhelmed, and that is the hard part. The radial Reynolds number stays of order one all the way down, so diffusion keeps competing with the inflow and the core evolves as a self-similar shape instead of simply collapsing. The speed grows without bound while the core's kinetic energy goes to zero.

Physically, the equations are not predicting an infinitely fast fluid. They are predicting their own boundary. Long before the singular time the core is narrower than the mean free path of air molecules and moving past the speed of sound, so the continuum picture the equations rest on is already false. No simulation of a wing or an artery changes because of this.

And what was proved is the version that allows a smooth external force: statements C and D in Fefferman's official formulation. A and B, with no force at all, are what most people mean by the question. They are exactly as open as they were last week.

The route belongs to two mathematicians in Madrid. Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa spent years building singularities out of an infinite cascade of amplifying scales, fighting to keep the force regular. Fefferman, who wrote the official problem statement in 2000, calls them the heroes of the story. OpenAI's paper went out with 165 pages and 16 references, not one of them theirs. It now has 166 pages and 22 references, three of them theirs.

Ten thousand agents and a Lean certificate is the part everyone posted about. The shape is the part worth keeping.

Ninety years to write it down. About a minute to explain it.

On October 6, OpenAI published 719 mathematical manuscripts from an internal model, grouped into 372 families. Among them: a proof that the Riemann zeta function has no zeros with real part above 7/8, formalized in Lean, and a proof of the Hodge conjecture for CM abelian varieties.

Neither is the famous conjecture. The first is a much weaker statement than the Riemann hypothesis; the second covers a large but special class of varieties. About 42% of the main results are formalized, and the day after the release three manuscripts were withdrawn over a sign error.

Two weeks ago I wrote that the "hundred problems" announcement came without a list or proofs. Now there is a list, and a great deal to study. That makes the question I care about more concrete: when AI can produce results faster than we can read them, who decides which ones are worth understanding?

Open problems are not a finite inventory. Every result opens new questions, and choosing well is part of mathematical judgment. A system rewarded for closing problems has an incentive to pick the ones it can close. I see no reason to assume AI can never learn to choose well, but the evidence would come from following its choices, not from asking it.

A correct proof, an idea someone understands, and a good research priority need different evidence. I updated the article with the new release.

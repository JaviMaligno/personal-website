You tried ChatGPT a couple of years ago, it made things up, you decided this was overhyped nonsense, and you haven't been back since.

You were right at the time. The tool was worse, the promises were ridiculous, and the people telling you it would change everything couldn't say what it would change for YOU specifically. Forming that opinion was reasonable. It just went out of date.

Here's the part nobody says out loud: the reason you're not using it isn't that you're not technical, and it isn't that you're resistant to change. Usually it's that nobody has ever told you what you can ask it for. A model answers what you put to it and never mentions what you left out — so if you think it writes emails and makes things up, you'll have it write an email, it'll do that, and you'll conclude you were right. There is no error message for a question you never thought to ask.

But there's a second reason, and it's the more honest one.

Someone I know watched a colleague spend an afternoon on Google, wading through articles on tax accounting with France. They showed her: give it the client's situation and what you need to work out, and it answers directly — with links you can go and check, so you're not taking its word for it.

She thought it was great. Then she went back to Google and carried on exactly as before.

That isn't ignorance and it isn't stubbornness. It's that the old way already works, and the new way costs something today for a payoff that's only a promise. Switching is a real cost and everyone underestimates it, especially the people doing the demonstrating.

The people in your company getting a lot out of this aren't smarter than you. They stumbled into a wider idea of what's possible, usually by accident, and everything follows from that.

So here's the only suggestion I have, and it takes an hour, once.

Take something you actually did this week — the thing you postponed, the report nobody wants to write, the forty emails you had to summarise. Not a demo. That thing.

Describe it in plain words, the way you'd explain it to a new colleague. Then ask what it would need from you to help, and whether there's a better way to approach it than the one you had in mind.

You are not learning a tool. You're finding out what's in range.

If it's useless for your work, you'll know in an hour and you'll have earned the right to say so — which is more than most people who dismiss it can claim.

The hour is the point: not enough to learn a tool, and small enough to risk on something that might not work out.

---

And if you already use this stuff daily: you know someone who doesn't. They're not lazy and they're not stupid, and showing them again won't do it — watching someone else do a thing has never once made anybody change how they work. Get them to do one real thing themselves. That's the only version of this that sticks.

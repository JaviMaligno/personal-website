I was convinced that the prescriptive skills we pack into AI context—those rules files and playbooks telling models how to think—were getting in the way of frontier performance. My intuition was simple: these models are smart enough now that the scaffolding is dead weight. To check it, I ran ~640 model responses and ~170 agent runs across four axes, on six models from GPT-4o to Opus 5.

I was wrong about where the damage is, not about whether there is any. On bug fixing the scaffolding is simply inert: these models already fix the cause rather than the symptom, so a debugging skill has nothing to prevent. But in several places it actively hurts. Loading a planning skill made the agent hand back a plan instead of the code — on the weaker model, 2 deliveries out of 12 against 7 with no skill at all. In single-turn planning, the same skill made models lose track of what had and hadn't already happened, 81% down to 65%. That is not a tax on discipline. That is the scaffolding changing what the model thinks the job is.

Two things survive that I'd act on. A planning skill costs two to three times the output tokens on both models I tested, and I could not find the case where it earns that back — including in a test I designed for it to win, on decisions the repository doesn't settle. And the one result that didn't move at all: across 96 agent runs and two capability tiers, not one broke a domain rule the repository had written down, including on a request built to bait exactly that. Given somewhere to look, models look.

So the question for your agentic workflows isn't whether your playbook is well written. It's whether you can name what it buys — and whether it's quietly redefining the task while you pay for it.

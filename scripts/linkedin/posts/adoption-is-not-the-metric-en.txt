Every AI rollout I hear about produces the same shape. A small group uses it every day and gets a lot out of it. Some use it occasionally and badly. A large share never opened it at all.

Licences get bought, dashboards show adoption, and the organisation moves at exactly the speed it did before. Both things are true at once, which is why the meeting about it goes nowhere.

I agree with that diagnosis. What I don't agree with is the conclusion people draw from it — that half the organisation is never getting there.

That conclusion assumes the distance is general ability. It isn't. Watch what actually separates the person who gets a lot from the person who gets almost nothing, and it isn't talent or technical background. It's that one of them knows what can be asked for and the other doesn't.

The group that uses it badly is where I keep hearing this flattened into "they just don't prompt well", and that's rarely what's happening. What I actually see:

— They tried something real, it came back wrong, and they stopped. One bad result, and the conclusion was about the tool rather than about the attempt.
— They can't tell a good result from a plausible one, so nothing they get back teaches them anything.
— They take the first answer as final, because nobody told them this was meant to be a back-and-forth.
— They describe the task the way they'd write a ticket for themselves, leaving out everything obvious — which is exactly the context that was doing the work.
— Somebody showed them, they agreed it looked useful, and they carried on the old way anyway.

What ties those together isn't skill at phrasing. It's that when the thing fails, **they have no way of knowing whose fault it was.** Was that a bad request, a bad check, or a bad tool? Without an answer, blaming the tool is the reasonable move, and then you stop.

The person who never opened it is in the same position one step earlier. Neither has any sense of the range, and nothing corrects them, because a model responds to what you put in front of it and never mentions what you left out.

There's no error message for a question that never got asked. That's the whole gap, and it's a short list of concrete things rather than a career's worth of skill.

I don't think you close it with a rollout. Access was never the constraint — the licences are already there, unopened. You close it by showing people what's askable, using their own work, until they've done one useful thing with it themselves.

The pessimistic read treats "hasn't happened" as "can't happen". Those aren't the same, and the difference is worth a lot of money to whoever gets it right.

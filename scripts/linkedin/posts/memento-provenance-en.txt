Justin Skycak compares LLMs to the protagonist of Memento: no long-term memory, just an elaborate system of sticky notes. His conclusion is that your edge over them is what you've consolidated in your own head, and that you only get there by doing your reps.

I agree with the conclusion, but the film says something more specific.

Leonard's problem isn't that notes are a weak substitute for memory. It's that he trusts notes written by a past self who lacked context, and that other people (and he himself) exploit that. A note doesn't tell you who wrote it, when, or whether it's still true.

That's exactly what happens with agents. Their memory files, the project's CLAUDE.md, yesterday's summary: everything is a note without provenance. The agent reads them with the same confidence whether they're true today or stopped being true three weeks ago. Your value in that system isn't holding more context than the agent. It's noticing when a note no longer holds.

And that does require judgment you've consolidated yourself. Here I'd refine the reps idea: reps of what? Not necessarily typing code. Judgment comes from committing to an answer and finding out you were wrong. Reading what an agent did and forgetting it the next day doesn't build that, but it's not because you didn't write it. It's because nothing was at stake.

The fix is cheap: before you read the agent's diagnosis, its design or its fix, decide what you expect. If you got it right, you've confirmed something. If you got it wrong, that's the rep. Working with agents gives you more of those moments per day than writing everything by hand, as long as you take the first guess yourself.
